Sewer Camera Inspection in Chesterfield, MO

A pipe camera shows what is inside your sewer line, how deep it runs and whether it needs cleaning, repair or nothing at all.

A sewer line is buried, so most of what happens inside it is guesswork until a camera goes in. A sewer inspection in Chesterfield, MO sends a small waterproof camera down the line from a cleanout and shows the pipe wall on a monitor. You see roots, cracks, scale, joints and low spots as they actually are. It is the most direct way to answer whether a repeat backup is a dirty pipe or a damaged one.

Do you have a camera? What the work actually involves

Callers often ask first whether we have the equipment. The honest answer is to describe the work. A pipe inspection uses a camera head on a flexible push rod, with a light, a cable reel and a monitor. A transmitter in the head lets a locator on the surface mark the exact spot and read the depth.

We do not name a brand because the brand is not what matters. What matters is a clear picture, a way to record it and an operator who can explain it. If your line is blocked, we clear it enough for the lens to see. If the pipe is full of water or sludge, we say what the camera cannot show.

What the camera shows, and what each finding means

The picture is only useful once somebody reads it. These are the findings we see most, with the usual consequence for the line.

What appears on screenWhat it meansWhat usually follows
Fine white or brown roots at a jointA small opening where moisture escapesCut the roots and plan a spot repair or lining
Dark, rough wall with flaking materialScale or corrosion in cast ironCleaning, then repair or section replacement if the wall is thin
A pool of water along part of the runA belly, a dip where the pipe has settledMonitor if minor, or dig and regrade if it collects solids
A ledge at a jointAn offset where two pipes have shiftedSpot repair or, for slight offsets, cleaning and watching
A split along the pipe lengthA crack or break in clay or plasticRepair or lining, depending on length and shape
Smooth, clean pipe with steady flowA line in good shapeNo repair needed, and the clog may be a fixture issue

How a camera visit goes from cleanout to answer

1

Find the way in

A cleanout in the yard, basement or crawlspace is the usual entry. Without one, the camera can go in through a removed toilet.

2

Clear the lens

If the line is blocked or heavily coated, it is cleaned enough to see. A camera pushed into a pipe full of sludge shows nothing but sludge.

3

Feed the camera down the line

The operator watches the monitor, notes the footage count and stops at anything unusual. The head is pulled back slowly to get a second look.

4

Mark the problem on the ground

The locator above the camera head finds the exact spot on the surface and reads the depth. This tells us what a dig would involve.

5

Explain what we see

We talk through the footage with you in plain words. If we spot damage, we name the likely repair. If we do not, we tell you that too.

When a sewer inspection is worth booking

Before you buy a house

Lines are out of sight and the seller may not know their condition. Footage lets you ask for repairs or a price change before closing.

After repeat backups

Two clogs in a year is a pattern. A camera tells you whether the line needs repair or the problem is something you can change.

Before big landscaping or tree planting

Knowing where the line runs and how deep helps you place trees and patios away from it.

When a cable keeps stopping

A machine halting at the same distance means something is there. The camera shows whether it is a root mass, a joint or a collapse.

After a repair

A second camera pass confirms the new section is joined cleanly and the old line downstream is sound.

What a camera can and cannot tell you

A camera inspection is a visual check, not a guarantee. It shows the pipe wall as it is on the day of the visit.

What it can show

  • Roots, cracks, scale, grease and foreign objects
  • Offsets, bellies and joint condition
  • The footage distance to each problem
  • The surface location and depth with a locator

What it cannot show

  • Hairline cracks hidden under heavy scale or water
  • How the line will behave in the next heavy rain or root season
  • What lies beyond a full collapse or blockage
  • Exactly where your lateral meets the public main at the street

What you get after the camera comes out

We explain what we saw, mark the locations and say whether the line needs sewer line cleaning, repair or nothing. If the footage shows damage, the next page in the process is sewer line repair, where spot repair, lining and replacement are laid out.

We keep this honest. A sound line gets a sound report, even though that means no repair sale. For buyers in nearby towns like Town and Country, footage of a clear, flowing line is a useful thing to hold.

Footage and the City's lateral repair program

Worth knowing

The City of Chesterfield says the property owner is responsible for the lateral sewer service line. The City's Residential Sanitary Sewer Lateral Repair Program can cover certain repairs up to $15,000, but only after the line has been cabled by a licensed plumbing company or licensed drainlayer and it did not clear.

A camera inspection is not the same as the cabling the program asks for, so keep the cabling invoice. Footage can still help you explain where the problem sits. Ask the City's Department of Public Works whether your repair qualifies before you spend money.
