Most failed heaters fail in one of a few ways. Knowing which one you have decides whether a repair is worth paying for, so this page walks through the symptoms, the leak sources, the fuel types and the point where replacing the unit is the better call.

What your water heater is telling you

Homeowners usually describe the symptom, not the part. These are the patterns we hear most, and the first thing each one points to.

No hot water at all

On electric units this is often a tripped breaker, a failed upper heating element or a tripped high-limit switch. On gas units it is more often a pilot that went out or a worn thermocouple.

Hot for a few minutes, then cold

A failed lower element on an electric tank, or a heavy layer of sediment on the bottom of any tank, leaves only the top third of the tank heated.

Lukewarm water that never gets hot

A thermostat set too low is the free fix. A mixing valve problem or a dip tube that has broken down and sends cold water straight to the outlet are the paid ones.

Rumbling, popping or knocking

Water trapped under hardened sediment boils and bursts through it. The noise is a warning that the tank is working harder and wearing out faster.

Rust colored hot water

If only the hot side runs brown, the tank lining or the anode rod is the likely source. If both sides do, the cause is upstream in the pipes.

Water on the floor

This is the one that cannot wait. The source matters, and the next sections show how to tell a harmless drip from a tank that is finished.

When a leaking water heater is an emergency

A tank that is leaking from the body or the bottom seam is failing from the inside, and the leak will grow. A fitting that weeps a few drops is a smaller job. If you cannot tell which you have and the floor is wet, treat it as the first kind and call the 24/7 line.

Water and a gas burner or a 240 volt element do not mix. If the floor around an electric heater is flooded, stay out of the water and shut off power only if the panel is dry and you can reach it safely. If you smell gas at any point, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 before you call us.

Electric, gas and hybrid heaters fail in different ways

An electric tank has two heating elements and two thermostats, and nearly every no-hot-water call comes back to one of them. We test each element for resistance with a multimeter and check the upper thermostat and the red reset button before condemning anything. An element is an inexpensive part, so this repair almost always makes sense on a tank that is otherwise sound.

A gas tank depends on a pilot or an igniter, a thermocouple or flame sensor, a gas control valve and a clear flue. A pilot that keeps blowing out points to a thermocouple that is worn or a draft problem in the vent. A burner that lights and shuts off points to a sensor. The gas control valve is the costly part, and on an older tank it can cost enough that replacement is the honest answer.

A hybrid or heat pump water heater adds a compressor, a fan, an air filter and a condensate drain on top of an electric tank. Many fault codes on these units trace to a blocked filter or a clogged condensate line, both of which are simple. A failed compressor or sealed system is another matter, because the repair can approach the price of a new unit, so we explain that before any work.

How we find the failure before quoting the fix

1

Safe shutdown and listening

We close the supply, secure the fuel and listen for rumbling, hissing or dripping. Sound often separates a sediment problem from a leaking fitting.

2

Dry and trace

We dry the unit and the floor, then watch where the first drop forms. Water travels along pipes and insulation, so the wettest spot is rarely the source.

3

Test the parts

A multimeter checks elements and thermostats on electric units. A manometer or draft test checks gas combustion and venting. A pressure gauge on a hose bib checks whether street pressure is high enough to trip the relief valve.

4

Check the tank itself

We look at the age on the data plate, the condition of the anode rod if it is accessible, and the sediment that comes out when a little water is drawn from the drain valve.

5

Explain the options

You hear the cause, the repair, what it costs, and whether replacement is wiser. The price is clear before we start work.

What Chesterfield water does to a tank

Worth knowing

Missouri American Water supplies most of St. Louis County. In its 2021 St. Louis region water quality report, hardness at the Missouri River plants ran from 76 to 170 mg/L as calcium carbonate, about 4.4 to 9.9 grains per gallon. Meramec River plants ran 66 to 148 mg/L, about 3.9 to 8.7 grains. That is moderately hard water, and heating drops the minerals out as scale.

In practice that scale settles on the tank bottom, insulates the burner or coats electric elements until they burn out. It is the reason a yearly flush helps and why the anode rod deserves a look every few years. Ask Missouri American Water for its latest report and which plant serves your address, or add a water softener or filter if scale is a repeat problem.

Repair it or replace it

Repair is the right call more often than people expect. The decision rests on the part that failed, the age of the tank and whether the tank itself is sound.

Repair makes sense when

  • A heating element, thermostat, thermocouple or relief valve is the failed part
  • The tank is in its first decade and shows no rust at the seams
  • The leak is at a fitting, valve or connector and not the tank wall
  • The anode rod can still be reached and is not fully consumed
  • The repair costs well under half the price of a new unit

Replacement makes sense when

  • Water is coming from the tank body or the bottom seam
  • The tank is well past ten years and has had a repair already
  • The gas control valve or a heat pump compressor has failed on an older unit
  • Sediment is so heavy the drain valve clogs and the flush fails
  • Rust is visible at the base and the floor has been wet more than once
