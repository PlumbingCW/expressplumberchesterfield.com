Which Tankless Water Heater Do You Need in Chesterfield, MO?

The right model depends on your fuel, venting path, gas line, electrical panel and how much space you have.

The tankless water heater you need depends less on brand than on four things your house already has: a fuel source, a place to vent exhaust, enough gas or electrical capacity and room on the wall. In Chesterfield, MO, most homeowners end up choosing between a gas condensing unit, a gas non-condensing unit or an electric unit, with a small point-of-use heater as a fourth option for a single far bathroom.

Each type has trade-offs in installation cost, running cost and upkeep. Below is a side-by-side comparison, then the practical checks that decide whether a model will fit: venting, gas line size, panel capacity and maintenance.

The four main types side by side

Use this as a starting map. Final selection needs a look at your home.

TypeBest forMain trade-off
Gas, condensingWhole house with high demandNeeds a condensate drain and a gas line that can carry the load
Gas, non-condensingWhole house where an existing metal vent is usableHotter exhaust needs stainless or specified vent material
Electric, whole houseHomes without gas or a good vent pathLarge electrical draw and breaker space
Electric, point-of-useOne bathroom, bar sink or a far fixtureLimited flow, not meant for the whole house

Flow, climate, fuel prices and local rules all shift the best choice, so treat this as a map, not a verdict.

Condensing versus non-condensing gas units

Condensing gas

  • Captures more heat from exhaust, so it uses less gas for the same hot water
  • Exhaust is cooler, so it can often vent through plastic pipe such as PVC, CPVC or polypropylene, depending on the model
  • Produces acidic condensate that must drain to a floor drain, a condensate neutralizer or similar
  • Typically costs more to buy

Non-condensing gas

  • Lower purchase price in many cases
  • Exhaust is hot, so venting usually needs stainless steel or another approved material
  • No condensate to drain
  • Uses more gas for the same output and often has a shorter efficiency edge

The four site checks that decide which unit fits

A model that looks perfect on paper can fail at the first of these.

Venting path

The exhaust needs a straight, short run through a wall or roof with the right clearances. A long path may limit your choices.

Gas line size

Tankless units draw far more gas at once than a tank heater, so an old line sized for a tank may need upsizing.

Electrical panel capacity

Whole-house electric units can draw well over 100 amps combined, so panel space and service size matter.

Wall space and clearances

Units mount on a wall with required distances from combustibles, windows and each other.

Water quality

Harder water scales the heat exchanger, so plan for descaling or a treatment device.

Gas line upsizing and why it surprises people

A tank water heater burns gas slowly and steadily. A tankless unit fires hard the moment you open a tap. That jump in demand is why a gas line sized for a tank often needs a larger pipe from the meter or a larger regulator.

Other gas appliances share the same line, so the plumber must add up the furnace, range and dryer too. If the line is too small, the unit shuts down or shows errors under load. Ask for the gas line check before you pick a model, since upsizing can add as much to the project as the heater itself. Our plumbing installation team handles gas piping work along with the unit.

Electric units and the panel question

An electric tankless heater has no flame, no vent and no gas line. That sounds simple until you read the amperage. Big whole-house units may need several double-pole breakers and heavy wire, and some homes simply do not have the spare capacity.

Smaller electric units suit a single bathroom or a bar sink. If you have a gas furnace and a gas range, a gas unit often fits the house better. If you have no gas, electric is the only tankless route, and an electrician should check the panel first.

Maintenance you sign up for with any tankless unit

Every tankless heater needs periodic flushing to remove scale, and the manufacturer's manual tells you how often. Many units have service ports for a descaling flush with vinegar or a descaling solution, and an inlet screen to clean. Skipping this step is the most common reason a good unit fails early.
