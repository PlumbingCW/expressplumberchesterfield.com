Signs you have a hidden leak

Some leaks announce themselves. Most do not. These are the signals worth acting on.

The meter moves with everything off

A dial or a small triangle that spins when no water is in use means water is leaving the system somewhere between the meter and your fixtures.

The water bill jumps with no change in habits

A bill that rises for a month or two without guests, a new pool or a lawn is a leak until proven otherwise.

Stains on ceilings and walls

Brown rings, bubbled paint or soft drywall mean water has been present long enough to soak in. Active leaks usually grow after use.

A warm or damp spot on the floor

A patch of floor that is warm underfoot can point to a hot water line running in or under the slab.

The sound of running water in a quiet house

A faint hiss or trickle at night, with all fixtures closed, points to a pressurized line leaking inside a wall or under the floor.

Low pressure or a musty smell

A drop in pressure, mold odor or a sudden patch of wet or green lawn along the path of the water line deserves a look.

How to confirm a leak yourself with a meter test

This takes about an hour and costs nothing. It tells you whether water is leaking past your meter before you spend on a search.

  • ✓
    Turn everything offClose every tap, pause the dishwasher and washing machine, shut off the ice maker, turn off irrigation and make sure no one flushes.
  • ✓
    Find the meterIt sits in a box near the street or in the basement or garage. Lift the lid carefully, since spiders and soil are common.
  • ✓
    Read the low-flow indicatorLook at the small dial or triangle. If it spins with all water off, water is moving.
  • ✓
    Note the reading and waitWrite down the numbers, wait 30 to 60 minutes without using water, and read again.
  • ✓
    Close the main and repeatIf the dial still moves with your main shutoff closed, the leak is in the line between the meter and the house. If it stops, the leak is inside the house.
  • ✓
    Rule out the usual suspectsAdd a few drops of food coloring to each toilet tank and wait 15 minutes. If color reaches the bowl, the flapper is leaking and that may be the whole story.

When a hidden leak is an emergency

A leak becomes urgent when water is damaging something right now: a ceiling bulging with water, a wet wall near outlets, a basement floor that is spreading, or a bill that suggests hundreds of gallons a day. Close the main shutoff, open a low faucet to bleed pressure, and call the 24/7 line.

Never push on a bulging ceiling or stand beneath it. A wet ceiling with a light fixture can be an electrical hazard, so keep the lights off, and shut off the breaker only if the panel is dry and reachable.

Water meter still running or a high water bill

Your meter is the most honest instrument in the house. If the low-flow indicator moves with all water off, you have a confirmed leak. The first split is whether it is inside the house or on the line outside, and the closed-main test above answers that.

A high bill with a meter that has stopped spinning points to something else: a toilet that runs on and off, an irrigation zone, a softener regeneration, a pool refill, or a billing error. Check those, and ask your water utility how it estimated the bill. Missouri American Water can tell you whether your usage pattern looks like continuous flow.

Ceiling and wall leaks

Water rarely appears where it starts. It runs along a joist or a pipe and stains the ceiling several feet away. We use a moisture meter, pin and pinless, to map the wet area, then a thermal imaging camera to see temperature differences that show where moisture sits behind drywall.

A ceiling stain under a bathroom usually points to a shower pan, a tub drain, a supply line or a toilet flange. Under a kitchen it often points to a dishwasher line or sink drain. Roof leaks and condensation from an air handler also mimic plumbing leaks, so we rule those out before cutting. The aim is a small, accurate opening, not a large one.

Leaks under the house, in the crawlspace, basement or slab

In a crawlspace or an unfinished basement, a lot of the pipe is visible. We check for drips, wet insulation, rust streaks and fresh mineral stains, then follow the line with a flashlight and a moisture meter. This is the cheapest kind of leak to find and the cheapest to fix.

Slab leaks are the hardest. Slab leak detection is covered here as a section, not a separate service. Pipes in or beneath a concrete floor are found with acoustic listening and thermal tools, not by guesswork. A hot water line leaking under a slab may warm the floor and raise your gas or electric bill. A cold line leak shows as a meter that runs and sometimes a damp patch.

Once the line is isolated and the sound located, we mark the floor so any repair can be a small opening. Depending on the damage, the options are an access repair, rerouting the line overhead, or in older homes with repeated failures, repiping.

Underground leaks on the water service line from meter to house

The service line runs from the meter, often at the curb, to the house. It is buried, so a leak shows up as a spinning meter with a closed main, a soggy patch of lawn, a pressure drop or a sinkhole-like depression. It may also show up as nothing at all, because soil drains water quietly.

We confirm the line is the culprit by shutting the main and watching the meter. Then a ground microphone and acoustic correlator listen along the line for the hiss of escaping water. A pipe locator traces the route, and a pressure gauge on a hose bib shows whether the line holds pressure. Tracer gas can help when the sound is faint.

Who owns which part of the line varies, so ask Missouri American Water where its responsibility ends and yours begins before you dig. A buried leak can be repaired in a short trench or replaced from end to end, depending on pipe condition.

Yard, irrigation, meter box and pool leaks

Irrigation leaks are common because lines are shallow and valves are buried in boxes. A stuck zone valve, a cracked head or a broken fitting can run all day. Turn the system off, repeat the meter test, and look for pooling near heads. In winter, drain and shut down the system, since frozen irrigation lines crack.

Meter box leaks happen at the fittings on either side of the meter. If the box is full of water, the meter dial keeps moving, or you hear a hiss at the curb, the leak may be on the utility's side or yours. Call Missouri American Water and describe what you see.

If you have a pool or spa, a leak in the plumbing lines is a separate case from a leak in the shell, and the bucket test tells them apart. We handle the house side of the plumbing, so ask a pool specialist about the shell.

Why low-lying Chesterfield property can fool a leak search

Worth knowing

Chesterfield Valley is low Missouri River floodplain. During the Great Flood of 1993, the Monarch levee gave way the night of July 30, and about 4,000 acres of the Valley flooded to depths of 6 to 15 feet. The Valley is now protected by the Monarch-Chesterfield Levee.

For a basement or slab in low ground, that history matters when water shows up where it should not. Groundwater, a failed sump pump or a drain problem can mimic a pipe leak, so we run the meter test first. A meter that stays still points away from the supply pipes toward drainage, and a sump pump or basement waterproofing may be the real fix.

When leak detection is not the right step

If water is visibly spraying from a pipe, skip the search and go straight to burst pipe repair. If the leak is at a water heater, start with water heater repair. And if the meter stays still and the symptoms point to a drain or the roof, detection will not find anything and we will say so.

We also do not open walls on a hunch. If the evidence is thin, we monitor with a pressure test and recheck before cutting. Homeowners in Wildwood and other nearby towns get the same process.
