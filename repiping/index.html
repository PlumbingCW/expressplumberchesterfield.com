One pinhole leak is a repair. A second one in a different room six months later is a pattern. Repiping in Chesterfield, MO starts when the pipes themselves, not a single joint, are the problem, and the water coming from the tap is often the first clue.

Brown or rusty water after sitting overnight, pressure that falls when two fixtures run, and green crust on copper joints all point toward the supply lines. We find the cause first. Replacing every pipe in a house is a large job, and it should only happen when spot repairs have stopped making sense.

Signs the supply pipes may be failing

Any one of these can have a smaller cause. Together they usually point to old or damaged supply lines.

Rusty or brown water

Water that runs orange or brown for the first few seconds, especially after the house has been empty, suggests corroding steel or iron pipe.

Pressure drops when two things run

Mineral and rust buildup narrows old galvanized pipe from the inside, so a shower slows when the washer fills.

Pinhole leaks in different spots

A damp ceiling stain, a wet baseboard, then another leak elsewhere. Copper can fail from the inside in a pattern called pitting.

Metallic taste or smell

Corroded pipe can change the taste. A test of your tap water can show what is in it.

Stains, bulges or flaking on exposed pipe

Visible rust, white mineral crust or green corrosion on joints means the metal is wearing out.

How we decide whether you need a repipe

A diagnosis comes before any quote. We shut off the water, put a pressure gauge on an outside hose bib, and watch what the pressure does when other fixtures open. Low pressure at the street shows up at every tap, while low pressure only in old pipe runs shows up where the pipe is narrowed.

We also unscrew an aerator, check for scale or rust flakes and look at the pipe where it is exposed in a basement or crawl space. Where a leak is hidden, a moisture meter or an acoustic listening device helps find it before any wall is opened. Our leak detection page explains that process.

If a single section has failed and the rest of the pipe looks sound, we say so and repair that run. A full repipe is the right answer when the metal is failing all over, not when one elbow cracked.

PEX or copper: an honest comparison

Both materials work and neither is right for every house. Here is how they compare.

PEX

  • Flexible tubing that bends around framing, so less wall opening
  • Resists splitting from a freeze better than rigid pipe, but not immune
  • Faster to install, which usually trims labor
  • Must not be exposed to sunlight, and some chlorine and fuel exposures are a concern

Copper

  • Long-used metal pipe with a strong track record
  • Takes heat well and does not degrade in sunlight
  • Soldered joints take more time and a skilled hand
  • Material is costly, and aggressive water can cause pitting over decades

How a repipe goes, room by room

The work is organized so the house stays usable for as much of it as possible.

1

Walk the house

We trace where supply lines run, mark fixtures and decide where access holes make sense. Plans differ in a slab house, a basement house and a two-story.

2

Shut off and drain

The main is closed, the system drained and the old water heater connections are noted before cutting.

3

Run new lines

New lines are routed through basements, crawl spaces and attics first. In-wall runs reach fixtures through small access cuts.

4

Connect fixtures and test

Each fixture gets a new stop valve and supply line. The system is pressure tested before any wall is closed.

5

Patch the openings

Drywall openings are patched and prepared for paint. Finish painting and texture matching are usually separate trades.

Permits, licensing and your water utility

Worth knowing

Permit rules vary, so ask the City of Chesterfield or St. Louis County before work that may need a permit. Plumber licensing in Missouri is set locally, so confirm any plumber's licence with the building department where the job is. If low pressure is on the street side, ask Missouri American Water whether the problem is at the main or inside your house.
