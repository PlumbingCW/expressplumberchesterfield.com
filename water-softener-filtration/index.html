White crust around the faucet, a cloudy film on the glass shower door and a water heater that sounds like popcorn all suggest hard water. Before you shop for a water softener installation in Chesterfield, MO, find out how hard your water really is, because that number sets the size and the price.

Most St. Louis County homes get water from Missouri American Water. Its 2021 St. Louis region report lists hardness in a range, not one figure for every house. We help you read it, test your own tap, and decide whether a softener, a sink filter or nothing at all is the sensible next step.

Signs the water is hard, and signs it is something else

Scale is the visible side of hardness. Other complaints that look similar need different equipment, so sort them out first.

Chalky crust on fixtures and aerators

Dissolved calcium and magnesium dry into scale. It clogs aerators and showerheads and builds up on heating elements.

Soap that will not lather and dull laundry

Hard water fights with soap and leaves residue on skin and fabric.

Water heater that rumbles or runs out early

Scale collects at the bottom of a tank and coats the heating element. A flush helps, though a softener slows the return.

Taste or smell of chlorine

This is not hardness. A softener will not remove it, but a carbon filter will.

Brown or orange staining

Iron or old pipe rust. Some softeners handle small amounts of iron, but heavy staining needs its own treatment.

What the 2021 water quality report says about hardness

Missouri American Water's St. Louis region report for 2021 says about 80 percent of the surface water comes from the Missouri River and about 20 percent from the Meramec River. Hardness at the Missouri River facilities ran from 76 to 170 mg/L as calcium carbonate, which is about 4.4 to 9.9 grains per gallon. At the Meramec River facilities it ran from 66 to 148 mg/L, or about 3.9 to 8.7 grains per gallon.

Those are 2021 numbers, and your own address could sit anywhere inside or beside those ranges. Ask Missouri American Water for the latest report and which treatment plant serves your address. Then check your own tap with a test kit so you size the equipment for real water, not a regional average.

Grains per gallon, milligrams per liter and what they feel like

Reports use mg/L while softener sellers talk in grains per gallon. One grain per gallon equals 17.1 mg/L, so you divide the report figure by 17.1.

Hardness (mg/L)Grains per gallonWhat you tend to notice
Under about 60Under about 3.5Little visible scale
About 60 to 120About 3.5 to 7Spots on glassware, slow scale on kettles and aerators

Bands follow the common soft, moderate, hard and very hard classes, not a regulatory limit. The 2021 report ranges above sit in the middle two rows, which is why testing your own tap matters.

How we size and install a softener

1

Test the water at your tap

We use a hardness test strip or titration kit and, if iron is a concern, an iron test as well.

2

Count the household

People and daily water use drive how many grains of hardness the unit has to remove each day.

3

Do the capacity math

Daily gallons times grains per gallon gives grains per day. A household using 300 gallons at 8 grains per gallon removes about 2,400 grains a day. The unit should hold several days of that before it regenerates.

4

Pick the location

The unit needs a drain for the backwash, a power outlet nearby and a spot where the bypass valve is reachable.

5

Install and plumb in

We set the unit, add a bypass, connect the drain with an air gap and check for leaks before programming it.

6

Set hardness and test the result

We program the valve to your measured hardness and test the treated water. A whole-house unit softens hot and cold, and many people leave one outside tap on hard water for the lawn.

Salt softener or salt-free conditioner

The word conditioner makes this confusing. Only one type actually removes the hardness minerals.

Salt-based softener

  • Removes calcium and magnesium through ion exchange
  • Needs salt or potassium chloride refills
  • Uses water and a drain for regeneration
  • Adds a small amount of sodium to the water
  • Gives soft water with no scale

Salt-free conditioner

  • Does not remove the minerals, it changes how scale forms
  • No salt and no drain connection
  • Reduces how hard scale sticks to surfaces
  • Does not give the slippery feel of soft water
  • Results vary by water and equipment

Reverse osmosis at the sink and whole-house filters

A reverse osmosis unit under the kitchen sink filters drinking water through a membrane, with a small tank and its own faucet. It improves taste and reduces many dissolved solids, but it only treats that one tap, and it sends some water to the drain. For taste or odor in the whole house, a carbon filter ahead of the water heater is the more common answer.

A softener and a filter do different jobs. A softener handles hardness, a filter handles sediment, taste and odor, and neither one removes everything. Tell us your goal and we will say which piece you actually need. Larger systems often pair with new plumbing installation work when a house is being updated.

What a softener will not fix

Buying the wrong equipment is the most common regret. A softener does not do these things.

  • ✓
    Remove chlorine taste or odor. That needs carbon filtration.
  • ✓
    Make water safe if it tests positive for bacteria.
  • ✓
    Fix low water pressure. Clogged pipes and a failing regulator are separate problems.
  • ✓
    Remove lead. Ask a lab or your water utility about testing.
  • ✓
    Clear scale already built up inside old pipes and tank water heaters.
  • ✓
    Solve a sewer smell or a rotten egg odor from a water heater.

Repairing a softener that has stopped working

Many softeners are still worth fixing. We service existing units and the controller valves on them.

Hard water despite a full salt tank

Salt can bridge into a crust above the water. A broken crust, a bad injector or a stuck valve is often the cause.

Valve will not cycle

A worn motor, stuck piston or failed timer keeps the unit from regenerating. We test the valve before we replace it.

Water or salt mush in the brine tank

A clogged line, a failed float or a bad injector can leave the tank overfilled.

Resin worn out

Resin beads lose capacity over many years. If the unit is old and the resin is gone, replacing the unit may cost about the same as a new tank of resin.
