Winter in Wildwood: the freeze that splits a pipe at 3 a.m.

Frozen supply lines in crawl spaces, garages and along north walls start most January calls. Missouri American Water's winter guidance is simple: know where your main shutoff valve is, test it, insulate exposed pipes, open cabinet doors under sinks and let a trickle run in sustained freezing weather. Set the thermostat no lower than 55 degrees when you are away.

If a pipe does freeze, shut off the main first. Warm the surrounding air gently, never use an open flame, and never leave a space heater unattended. Restore water slowly and check every joint for drips. If a pipe has already split, call the emergency line right away.

If a Wildwood pipe has already frozen or split

1

Close the main

Turn off the shutoff near the meter or where the line enters the house. Test it before winter so you know it works.

2

Open the faucets

Open the tap closest to the frozen section and the lowest tap in the house to let pressure out as it thaws.

3

Warm the air, not the pipe with flame

A hair dryer on low or a heater with someone watching it is safe. A torch is not.

4

Check every joint

Turn water back on slowly and walk the line. A thawed pipe may leak at a split you could not see frozen.

5

Call for a leak check

If water appears anywhere unexpected, call the 24/7 line. We locate the break with a pressure gauge or an acoustic detector instead of opening walls blind.

Spring rain, sump pumps and sewer backups

Steady spring rain loads storm drains and soaks the ground around foundations. In a lower level it can show up as a damp slab, a pit that never stops cycling, or a floor drain that bubbles. A working pump with a backup is the first defense, and our sump pump service covers repair and battery systems.

Slow drains in every fixture after a storm point to a blockage or a lateral problem, not the weather. Tell us which fixtures back up and whether the toilets gurgle. That tells us whether to clear a drain or start with a camera.

Summer irrigation, outdoor taps and the lawn that stays wet

Warm months bring irrigation, hoses and hot-water demand. These are the calls we see.

A buried supply leak under the lawn. A meter that moves with everything off confirms it, and leak detection pinpoints the spot.

Low pressure in the whole house

A leak, a closed valve, or a failing pressure regulator. We check the gauge at a hose bib.

Irrigation line hits
