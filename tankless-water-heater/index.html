The shower runs hot, then lukewarm, then hot again. The display flashes a code you have never seen. Or the water barely trickles from the hot side while the cold runs fine. Tankless symptoms and what usually causes them

A tankless unit has no tank to blame, so each symptom points to a short list of parts. This table is the starting point for our diagnosis.

What you noticeCommon causesFirst step
Water runs hot, drops to lukewarm, then hot againFlow below the unit's minimum, scale on the heat exchanger, or a mixing issueTry a higher flow at one fixture and see if it steadies, then plan a descale
Error code on the displayIgnition failure, flame sensor, exhaust or venting fault, high limit from scaleLook the code up in the manual, power cycle once, then call if it returns
Weak flow from hot taps onlyClogged inlet filter screen or scaled heat exchangerClean the inlet screen, which is a safe homeowner step on most models
Water never gets hot enough in winterUnit is undersized for the temperature rise, or one fixture is draining more than expectedCheck the setpoint, then ask whether the unit matches the house
Takes long for hot water to arriveLong pipe run from the unit to the fixture, not a heater faultTime it, then discuss a recirculation option
Unit shuts off while runningBlocked vent or condensate drain, low gas supply, or an overheating exchangerCheck the vent termination for debris and call if it persists

If you smell gas near the unit, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 before anything else. A fault code is never worth staying in a gas smell for.

How we find the cause of a tankless problem

1

Read the code and history

Many units store recent faults. The code and the pattern tell us whether this is combustion, flow, temperature or venting.

2

Check flow and temperature rise

We measure the inlet and outlet temperature while one tap runs at a set flow. A low rise at full flow means scale or a failing burner or element.

3

Inspect the vent and gas or power supply

On gas units we check the vent termination, the gas pressure and the condensate line. On electric units we check the breakers and the incoming voltage.

4

Pull the inlet filter and look inside

A screen packed with grit explains a lot, and a white crust on the exchanger explains the rest.

5

Decide: flush, repair or replace

You hear the cause, the cost and an honest view on whether the unit has more life in it.

Tankless water heater flush and descaling

Scale is the number one service issue on tankless units. The heat exchanger is a narrow passage, and a thin crust cuts flow, raises exit temperature and trips high limit codes. A flush circulates a descaling solution, usually white vinegar or a manufacturer approved product, through the exchanger with a small pump and hoses connected to isolation valves.

A flush is only possible if the unit has service valves on the hot and cold lines. If yours does not, we add them first, because without them the cleaning is guesswork. After circulating for the time your manual specifies, we rinse with clean water, clean the inlet screen and test output temperature.

Flushing helps more than it harms. But if scale has already cracked a heat exchanger or burned out a flow sensor, descaling will not repair that damage, and you may be looking at a part replacement or a new unit.

Tankless unit throwing errors?

Scale from hard water causes many of them, and a descale often brings the unit back.

Water in that range will form scale. Many manufacturers call for a yearly flush in hard water areas, and some units with a lot of use benefit from sooner. Read your manual, and consider a water softener if you are descaling often. A softener also protects the faucets and appliances downstream.

Gas versus electric tankless: what each needs

The fuel decides the install cost and the maintenance. Both heat water well when the house supports them.

Gas tankless

  • Heats large flows and can serve several fixtures at once
  • Needs a gas line sized for the unit's burner, often larger than a tank needs
  • Needs a sealed vent through a wall or roof, and a condensate drain on condensing models
  • Has combustion parts that need checking, and a burner to keep clean
  • Do not run it with a blocked vent

Electric tankless

  • No vent and no gas, so placement is flexible
  • Needs heavy electrical service, sometimes several breakers, and may need a panel upgrade
  • Best for point of use or a house with modest hot water demand
  • Coils can fail from scale and are cheaper to replace than a gas exchanger
  • Winter incoming water is cold, so the unit has to work harder

Venting, minimum flow and sizing basics

These three things trip up more installs than the brand does.

Venting

Condensing gas units can vent in plastic pipe through a side wall. Non-condensing units run hot exhaust and need stainless vent. Terminations need clearance from windows, doors and meters.

Minimum flow

Tankless units need a minimum flow to fire. A trickle at a low-flow fixture may not turn the burner on, which is why some taps run cold when barely open.

Sizing

Capacity is set by how much hot water you use at once and how cold the incoming water is. In Missouri winters, the incoming temperature is the part people underestimate.

Hot water lag

The wait for hot water depends on pipe length from the unit, not the heater. A recirculation line shortens it.

Honest limits on tankless repair

Not every brand or model is practical to repair. Some boards, heat exchangers and proprietary sensors are expensive, sold only through the manufacturer or discontinued. When a repair approaches the price of a new unit, we say so and show your options.

Tankless is also not the right answer for every home. If your gas line is small, venting would require a major rebuild, or a large household runs several showers at once, a storage tank may serve you better. If your tankless water heater is leaking, treat it like any other leak: shut off the water and fuel and call the 24/7 line.
