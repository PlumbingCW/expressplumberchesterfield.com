Tankless Water Heater Leaking in Chesterfield, MO

Find the source of a tankless leak, shut off the right supply and learn which causes are repairable and which are not.

A tankless water heater leaking in Chesterfield, MO usually comes down to one of five places. A loose fitting at the bottom, a clogged condensate drain, a cracked heat exchanger, a relief valve that is passing water or a scale buildup that has started eating through parts. Each has a different cost and a different urgency.

First, stop the water. Close the cold water shutoff valve below the unit, turn off the power at the switch or breaker and, for a gas model, close the gas valve. Then read on to work out what you are dealing with.

What to shut off, in this order

1

Close the cold water isolation valve

Most tankless units have service valves under the unit. Turn the cold side handle a quarter turn so it sits across the pipe. This stops fresh water from entering.

2

Close the hot isolation valve

Shutting the hot side stops water from the house flowing back through the unit if a pressure change pushes it.

3

Cut power

Switch off the unit and flip the breaker if it is dry and easy to reach. Do not stand in water to do it.

4

Close the gas valve on gas models

Turn the gas handle so it is crossways to the pipe. If you smell gas at any point, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 first.

5

Contain the water

Put a pan or towels down, then take photos of the leak point and any error code on the display before you call.

Where the leak comes from and what it means

Match what you see to the likely source. The color and timing of the water point to the cause.

What you seeLikely sourceRepair or replace
Drip at the pipe connections underneathLoose union, worn washer or failed fittingOften a repair: new gaskets or fittings
Slow drip from the pressure relief valve lineValve weeping from high pressure or debrisRepair: replace valve, check thermal expansion

Water running down the wall from above the unit may not be the heater at all. Check for a leaking pipe or a bad venting joint.

How we trace the leak instead of guessing

The first move is to dry the unit and every pipe around it, then watch the base for fresh water with a flashlight and a paper towel. A drip that returns within minutes tells you which fitting to open. A drip that only shows up after a hot water draw points to expansion or a valve.

We use a pressure gauge on the supply line to look at static pressure, because high pressure can cause a relief valve to weep without any part being broken. A moisture meter helps find hidden moisture in the wall behind the unit.

On condensing models, we look at the condensate trap and the neutralizer before touching anything else. These are the most common and cheapest fix, and a clogged one makes it look like the heater itself is failing.

The five usual suspects in more detail

Fittings and unions

Vibration, heat cycles and old washers loosen connections. If the drip is at a union, replacing the washer and tightening it correctly often ends the leak.

Condensate drain

High-efficiency units make acidic water as they cool exhaust gas. A blocked line sends that water back into the cabinet or onto the floor.

Pressure relief valve

A valve that drips usually responds to high water pressure or has debris on the seat. Replacing the valve is not a fix if the pressure is the real cause.

Heat exchanger

Hard water scale and freezing can crack the exchanger. Water leaking from the core of the unit rarely returns to service.

Scale and corrosion

Mineral buildup narrows the flow and attacks seals. It builds quietly for years and then shows up as a leak.

Chesterfield water hardness and what it does to a tankless unit

Chesterfield note

Missouri American Water supplies most of St. Louis County. In its 2021 St. Louis region water quality report, hardness at the Missouri River facilities ran from 76 to 170 mg/L as calcium carbonate, about 4.4 to 9.9 grains per gallon. The Meramec River facilities ran from 66 to 148 mg/L, or about 3.9 to 8.7 grains per gallon.

That is moderately hard to hard water, and it leaves scale on heating surfaces and in tankless heat exchangers. Scale cuts flow, overheats parts and can shorten the life of seals. Ask Missouri American Water for the latest report and which treatment plant serves your address, and think about a flush schedule or a water softener or filter for the line feeding the heater.

Source types: utility water quality report.

Fix it or replace it

Age matters less than the part that failed. This is how we think about it.

Repair makes sense when

  • The leak is at a fitting, union or valve
  • The condensate drain or neutralizer is blocked
  • The relief valve is weeping from pressure
  • Descaling brings flow back to normal
  • The unit is otherwise running well

Replacement makes sense when

  • Water comes from inside the heat exchanger
  • Repeated leaks follow one repair after another
  • Scale has damaged more than one part
  • Repair cost approaches the cost of a new unit
  • The unit cannot be serviced for a part

What changes the cost of a tankless leak repair

The cause, not the brand, sets the price. A union washer and a heat exchanger replacement are different jobs.

Source of the leakFittings and valves are minor. Internal parts and exchangers cost much more.
Access to the unitUnits mounted high on a wall or in a tight closet take longer to open up.
Scale conditionA heavy-scale unit may need a descaling visit before any repair holds.
Venting and gas line conditionCorroded vent parts or gas fittings may need work at the same time.
Parts availabilityCommon parts are on hand. Older models may need ordered parts, which takes time.

Homeowners policies commonly cover sudden accidental water damage but not wear or maintenance. Document the leak with photos and call your insurer if the water reached the floor or wall.
