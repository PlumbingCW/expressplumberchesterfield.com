What a failing pump looks like before the basement floods

Most pumps give warning. The trouble is that the warning shows up as small oddities, and nobody checks a pit that has worked for years.

The motor hums but no water moves

A jammed impeller, an airlocked pump or a failed check valve can all sound like a working pump. The pit keeps filling anyway.

It runs far more often than it used to

Short, frequent cycles point to a check valve letting water fall back into the pit or a float that sticks. Both shorten pump life fast.

A loud rattle or grinding

Gravel, a worn bearing or a bent impeller shaft. A pump making this noise is close to failing, even if it still empties the pit today.

The pump never turns on

Start with power and the float. A float wedged against the pit wall or a dead switch is cheaper to fix than a motor.

Water stands in the pit with the pump running

The discharge line may be clogged, frozen or kinked, or the pump may be too small for the inflow.

How we find out why the pump stopped

1

Check power first

We test the outlet, the GFCI and the circuit with a meter. A surprising number of dead pumps are a tripped breaker after a power flicker.

2

Test the float and the switch

We lift the float by hand and watch whether the pump starts. A float that sticks is a cheap repair. A switch that fails sometimes is a reason to replace the pump.

3

Look at the pit and the impeller

Silt, gravel and debris in the bottom of the pit cause many jams. If the pump pulls out cleanly, we inspect the impeller and screen.

4

Follow the discharge line

We trace the pipe outside to confirm water is leaving the house and not draining back. A check valve that has failed is one of the most common finds.

5

Run a fill test

We add water to the pit and watch a full cycle. The pump should start, empty the pit and shut off without chattering.

6

Tell you what the repair is worth

If a pump is old and the motor is tired, we say so. Replacing it can cost less than a second emergency visit.

Why the Valley's 1993 flood still matters for pumps

Worth knowing

On the night of July 30, 1993, the Monarch agricultural levee gave way with the Missouri River more than 19 feet above flood stage. About 4,000 acres of Chesterfield Valley flooded to depths of 6 to 15 feet. The Valley had not flooded since 1935. About 240 businesses operated there beforehand and about 160 reopened.

Today the Valley sits behind the Monarch-Chesterfield Levee and is mostly offices and retail on former farmland. A levee keeps the river out. It does nothing for a power outage, a heavy local downpour or groundwater that rises against a slab. If your building has a pit, the pump and its discharge line are what protect you in the storms the levee was never meant to handle, so test them before the season, not during it.

One pump alone or a pump with battery backup

Storms often knock out power right when the pit needs the pump most. A backup system adds a second pump and a battery so the pit keeps emptying without the grid.

Primary pump only

  • Lower cost to install
  • Stops the moment house power drops
  • Fine where the pit seldom fills or flooding risk is low
  • No battery to test or replace

Primary pump plus battery backup

  • Keeps pumping through a storm outage
  • Needs a battery that must be tested and replaced over time
  • Runtime depends on how fast water enters the pit
  • Worth it where a flooded basement would damage finished space or equipment

Ejector pumps and when a new pump is not the answer

An ejector pump is a sealed unit that lifts waste from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the sewer line. A sump pump moves groundwater and storm seepage outside. They look alike but they are not interchangeable, and sump discharge should not be connected to the sanitary sewer. Ask the building department where the job is about local rules.

A new pump cannot fix a pit that is simply overwhelmed. If water is entering through the floor-wall joint or the foundation, basement waterproofing addresses where the water comes in. If the whole house drains slowly while the pit overflows, our drain cleaning crews can check whether the main line is the real problem.
