How to Install a Sump Pump Battery Backup in Chesterfield, MO

Pick the right backup, set it beside your primary pump and test it before the next storm takes the power out.

To install a battery backup for a sump pump in Chesterfield, MO, you set a second pump in the pit, give it its own check valve and discharge, wire it to a deep-cycle battery with a charger and test it. Many homeowners can do the swap if the pit is large enough and the discharge is easy to reach.

The risk is the storm that knocks out power at the same moment the pit fills. A backup only helps if it sits at the right height, has a working float and its battery is charged. This guide walks through the choices and the order of work.

Primary pump versus backup pump

The primary pump is the AC pump that runs most of the year. The backup is a second pump that starts when the primary fails or when the water climbs higher than the primary can handle. It can run on battery power or on water pressure from the house supply.

A battery system has a controller, a deep-cycle battery and a charger. It will run a smaller pump for a limited time, often several hours of steady pumping or longer if cycling is light. A water-powered backup needs no battery but depends on municipal water pressure and sends extra water down the drain.

Neither is a substitute for a healthy primary pump. If the primary is old, noisy or running all the time, replace it first.

Choosing a backup that fits your pit

Size matters more than the label on the box. A backup pump moves less water than a large primary, so check its rated output at your discharge height. A pit with high inflow during storms needs a backup with a strong flow rate and a battery that can cycle often.

Look at the pit lid and the discharge height too. A long vertical lift to the outlet cuts the flow of any pump. If your discharge line runs uphill or far from the house, tell the seller or plumber so the pump can be matched.

Buy a deep-cycle battery rated for this kind of use. A standard car battery loses capacity when it is drained and recharged repeatedly.

Which backup type fits which house

Match the backup to how long power tends to stay off and how fast your pit fills.

Installing the backup pump, step by step

1

Check the pit size

Measure the pit width and depth. Two pumps need room to sit side by side with floats that do not tangle. A narrow pit may call for a combination unit or a new pit.

2

Cut power and clear the pit

Unplug the primary pump and remove standing water. Dry-fit the new pump on a flat base and check it clears the primary.

3

Set the backup higher than the primary

The backup float should trigger a couple of inches above the primary float so it only starts when the primary fails. Follow the manufacturer spacing.

4

Add a check valve and a separate discharge

Run a short pipe from the backup pump with its own check valve so water does not flow back into the pit. Tie it into the discharge pipe above the primary check valve.

5

Wire and mount the controller

Mount the controller and battery above floor level in a dry spot. Connect the pump leads, charger and alarm following the instructions.

6

Test it

Fill the pit with a bucket until the primary float rises, then unplug the primary and keep adding water. The backup should start. Repeat twice.

When DIY is fine and when to call

The wiring and plumbing involved are modest. The risk is what happens if it fails.

A confident homeowner can handle

  • Replacing a backup in an existing, roomy pit
  • Connecting a battery and charger to a ready kit
  • Adding a check valve to a straightforward discharge
  • Setting the float heights and testing the system
  • Replacing an aging battery on a schedule

Call a plumber when

  • The pit is too small or cracked
  • The discharge line is frozen, clogged or long
  • The primary pump runs constantly or short cycles
  • Wiring needs a new outlet or circuit
  • The basement has flooded before and you want a full plan

Why the Chesterfield Valley and the 1993 flood matter for backup power

Chesterfield note

Chesterfield Valley is low-lying Missouri River floodplain. In the Great Flood of 1993 the Monarch levee gave way on the night of July 30 and about 4,000 acres of the Valley flooded to depths of 6 to 15 feet. The Valley is now protected by the Monarch-Chesterfield Levee.

Nearby homes and buildings still deal with groundwater and storms, and storms are when power fails. A backup pump is a practical response to power loss and a high pit, not a guarantee against flooding. If your property sits low or has flooded before, size the pump with a margin and test it each spring. Ask the City or St. Louis County about permit rules before changing the discharge or adding electrical work.

Maintenance that keeps the backup working

  • ✓
    Test the backup pump twice a year by raising the float or filling the pit.
  • ✓
    Check the battery date and replace it according to the maker guidance.
  • ✓
    Keep the charger plugged in and the indicator light visible.
  • ✓
    Clean debris from the pit and look at both check valves.
  • ✓
    Make sure the discharge pipe is clear and the outlet is not frozen or buried.
  • ✓
    Review the alarm every few months and replace its batteries.
