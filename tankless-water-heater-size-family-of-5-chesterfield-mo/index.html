What Size Tankless Water Heater for a Family of 5 in Chesterfield, MO?

Sizing a tankless unit is arithmetic: add up the fixtures running together, then check the heat needed to warm the water.

For a family of five in Chesterfield, MO, most homes land on a tankless water heater rated for about 7 to 9 gallons per minute at the temperature rise they need. That is a rule of thumb, not a verdict. The real answer comes from two numbers: how many gallons per minute you run at the busiest moment, and how many degrees the unit must add.

Tankless sizing is not about family headcount alone. A house with two showers going while the dishwasher runs needs more than a house where five people shower at different times. Below is the fixture math with typical flow rates, a worked example, and the gas versus electric trade-offs.

Typical fixture flow rates you can add together

These are typical figures for modern fixtures, labeled as typical because your own may differ. Check the label on your showerheads and the dishwasher and washer manuals for the real numbers.

FixtureTypical flow (GPM)Notes
Shower2.0Many showerheads are rated at 2.5 or lower
Bathroom sink1.0Often a 1.0 to 1.5 aerator
Kitchen faucet1.5Higher if you open it fully for pots
Dishwasher1.5Draws hot water during fill cycles
Washing machine2.0Depends on cycle and model
Soaking tub fill4.0 or moreA large tub may be the biggest single draw

Flow rates are typical, not measured values for your home. Count only the fixtures that can run at the same time.

The worked example for a busy morning

Picture two showers running while the dishwasher finishes a cycle. That is 2.0 plus 2.0 plus 1.5, so 5.5 gallons per minute of hot water at once. Add a bathroom sink at 1.0 and you reach 6.5 GPM.

Next comes the temperature rise, which is the number of degrees the unit must add. Say the rise is 55 degrees, as an illustration only. The heat needed in BTU per hour is GPM times rise times 500, so 5.5 times 55 times 500 comes to about 151,000 BTU per hour.

That is a large number, and it explains why a family of five often needs a bigger unit than a catalog headline suggests. For electric, divide BTU per hour by 3,412 to get kilowatts, which is how the 44 kW figure below was reached. A smaller rise or lower flow shrinks both numbers quickly, so shorter showers and low-flow heads genuinely help.

Gas versus electric for a household this size

The same demand looks very different depending on the fuel.

Gas tankless

  • Whole-house units commonly top out around 199,000 BTU per hour, which covers many busy households
  • Needs proper venting and often a larger gas line than your old tank used
  • Handles two bathrooms running at the same time when sized correctly
  • Higher purchase and install cost, lower energy cost per gallon for many homes

Electric tankless

  • The same 5.5 GPM at a 55 degree rise needs roughly 44 kW, which is a big load for most panels
  • No vent and simple placement, but heavy wiring and large breakers
  • Works best for lower flow, point-of-use or a small second bathroom
  • Often needs a panel upgrade for a family of five if used as the only heater

What Chesterfield homeowners should check

Chesterfield note

Missouri American Water's 2021 St. Louis region report says about 80 percent of its surface water comes from the Missouri River and about 20 percent from the Meramec River. Surface water is colder in winter than water from deeper sources, so the temperature rise your heater must add is larger in January than in July. Size for the cold months, not the summer ones.

The same report gives Missouri River facility hardness of 76 to 170 mg/L as calcium carbonate, about 4.4 to 9.9 grains per gallon. That is hard enough to scale a tankless heat exchanger over time. Ask Missouri American Water for the latest report and which treatment plant serves your address, and plan for a descaling schedule.

Source types: utility water quality report, city website.

Two bathrooms at once: the case that breaks undersized units

When two showers and a kitchen draw overlap, an undersized unit cannot hold the temperature. It cuts back flow or the water turns lukewarm. That is not a defect. It is arithmetic. A heater that meets the rise at 4 GPM will fall short at 6.5 GPM, and the rise it can deliver drops as the flow goes up.

Two common fixes exist. One is a larger single unit. The other is two smaller ones, for example a whole-house unit plus a point-of-use heater for a far bathroom. Pipe length matters too, because a far fixture waits longer for hot water no matter how large the heater is. Our tankless water heater team can run the numbers with you.
